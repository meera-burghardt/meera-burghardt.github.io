Policy Topic: Devolution and Productivity within the UK

Devolution in the United Kingdom can be defined as the transfer of power from the national UK parliament to lower level governments. In England, this means more power granted to local and combined authorities, while in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland this entails transfer to national assemblies and parliaments.

In 1998, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland all devolved from the UK parliament, and since have been given broader legislative powers in relation to health, education, social welfare, and environmental policies. On the other hand, England does not have a unitary national devolved parliament, but rather a subset of combined authorities and directly elected mayors who have a say over housing, skills, and transport.

As the productivity levels in the UK have been stagnant over the last decade, the question arose of whether devolution could help stimulate productivity levels. In the subsequent analysis, I will analyze how devolution moves alongside productivity at the national, subnational, and regional levels over the last two decades within the UK.

Chart 1: National Level Productivity

Policy Implications

These charts analyze the association between productivity and devolution. As you can see in the first chart, Scotland Northern Ireland saw increases in productivity per hour relative to the UK average since 2004. The figure shows Scotland Northern Ireland on a positive trend from around 2010 onwards. On the other hand, Wales looks relatively stagnant at its 2004 productivity levels, while England excluding London declines throughout the past two decades. The result is a mixed picture: Scotland and Northern Ireland (two countries which devolved in 1999) saw increases in their productivity post 2010. while Wales and England (excluding London) remain relatively constant at their early 2000s levels. While this doesn't directly prove devolution increases productivity, it does show that, following devolution, two out of the four UK countries experienced increases in productivity relative to the national average.

Looking at the second chart, which breaks down this trend at the regional level, the results are mixed. Within the UK, some regions have seen increases in productivity while others have seen decreases. Further areas of research would be to analyze difference in productivity before and after devolution in English regions vs Scottish, Welsh, or Northern Irish ones.
